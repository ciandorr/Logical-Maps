Negative Self-Similarity evaluates Alternating St Petersburg

Claim. Rich Outcomes + Stochastic Equivalence + Simple EU + Uniqueness of Negative Self-Similarity imply Alternating St Petersburg =−1/2=-1/2.

Let AA be any gamble with

ℙ(u(A)=(−2)n)=2−n,n≥1.\mathbb P\bigl(u(A)=(-2)^n\bigr)=2^{-n},\qquad n\geq1.

Use numerical utility notation. Multiplication by −2-2 moves the nnth atom to the (n+1)(n+1)st value. Consequently

ℒ(A)=ℒ(M1/2(−2A,−2)).\mathcal L(A)=\mathcal L\bigl(M_{1/2}(-2A,-2)\bigr).

Indeed, the sure branch gives the first atom −2-2 probability 1/21/2, and the transformed branch gives each value (−2)m(-2)^m, m≥2m\geq2, probability 122−(m−1)=2−m\frac12\,2^{-(m-1)}=2^{-m}. Stochastic Equivalence therefore yields

$$A\sim M_{1/2}(-2A,-2). \tag{1}$$

Let cc be sure utility −1/2-1/2. Its corresponding mixture is

M1/2(−2c,−2)=M1/2(1,−2),M_{1/2}(-2c,-2)=M_{1/2}(1,-2),

which is simple and has expected utility −1/2-1/2. Simple EU gives

$$c\sim M_{1/2}(-2c,-2). \tag{2}$$

Equations (1) and (2) are two instances of the same fixed-point equation with p=1/2p=1/2, a=2a=2, b=0b=0 and ZZ sure −2-2. Uniqueness of Negative Self-Similarity therefore implies A∼cA\sim c, which is the desired evaluation.

Rich Outcomes ensures the availability of the sure values and transformed variables. The proof uses no finite expectation for AA, and it does not need Totality, Mixture Independence, Stochastic Dominance or affine invariance as additional assumptions.
